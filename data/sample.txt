The little workshop stood beside a quiet river. Each morning, Mira opened the windows and let the light fall across her desk. She kept a notebook for questions and a second notebook for answers. The questions were always longer than the answers.

One day, she decided to build a machine that could finish a sentence. She began with letters. A letter became a number, and a number became a small row of values. The machine saw a line of letters and tried to guess the next one. At first, every guess was strange.

Mira gave the machine more examples. The river was quiet. The workshop was warm. The notebook was open. She wrote these sentences in different ways, and the machine slowly noticed that some letters often followed others. A space often came after a word. A period often came after a thought.

Her friend Tomas asked how the machine remembered the order. Mira placed numbered stones on the desk. Each stone marked a place in the sentence. Without the stones, the machine could see the letters but could not easily tell which came first.

Then she made a rule: every place could look at itself and the places before it. No place could look ahead. If the machine could see the answer, it would learn to copy instead of learning to guess. Tomas nodded and covered the last word with his hand.

The machine asked three questions about each place. What am I looking for? What can I offer? What information should I pass along? Mira called these the query, the key, and the value. The names sounded grand, but the work was simple arithmetic.

Some places mattered more than others. The word river helped the machine choose water. The word workshop helped it choose tools. Each small group of values learned to notice a different kind of pattern. Together, the groups made a better guess than one group alone.

After each guess, Mira measured the mistake. A confident wrong answer cost more than a careful uncertain answer. She changed the values a little, then tried again. The machine learned through many small corrections, not through one sudden discovery.

She saved a few pages that the machine had never practiced on. These pages helped her see whether it was learning useful patterns or only remembering its lessons. Practice scores and new-page scores were both written in the notebook.

In the evening, Tomas gave the machine the words, The little. It replied, workshop. Another evening, it replied, river. Mira could make the guesses more predictable or more surprising. Neither setting gave the machine knowledge that was missing from its lessons.

The next morning, the windows were open again. The river moved past the workshop. Mira added another question to her notebook: what would happen if the lessons were larger, clearer, and more varied? She left space underneath for an answer.
